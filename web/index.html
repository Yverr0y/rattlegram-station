Hide in audio

Rattlegram is a loud, fragile signal: inside its 1600 Hz band it must be the stronger sound, so you can't bury it under louder music and still decode it. What you can do is make the two coexist — place the data where the music is quiet, or notch a hole for it.

What this is, and what it isn't

CapacityUp to 170 bytes per ~1 second burst. Emoji cost 4 bytes each.
Band1600 Hz wide, centred on the carrier. Default 1500 Hz → occupies 700–2300 Hz. Tunable 1000–3000 Hz, or up to ~20 kHz (near-ultrasonic) at 48 kHz.
SensitivityMeasured on this engine (AWGN, 2500 Hz ref): ≥90% decode at about +3 dB SNR for ≤85 B, +5 dB for ≤128 B, +7 dB for ≤170 B. It needs a positive SNR — roughly "if you could just talk over the link, it gets through."
PrivacyThe payload scrambler uses a fixed public seed. A plain rattlegram is readable by anyone with the app. Hiding it in music is obscurity, not secrecy. For real confidentiality, use the Encrypt option (AES-GCM) so eavesdroppers get only ciphertext.
RangeSet entirely by what carries the sound: a few metres speaker-to-mic; 1–10 km on handheld radios; 100s–1000s km on ham HF. The modem doesn't change this.
Law. On amateur radio bands, transmitting messages "encoded for the purpose of obscuring their meaning" is prohibited in many countries (e.g. US FCC Part 97 §97.113) — so the Encrypt option is for your own local/line-level/unlicensed audio between devices, not for ham transmissions. Many licence-free services (PMR446, FRS, CB) are voice-only or restrict data — check your local rules. These are open broadcasts; never send anything you need to keep private without your own encryption, and even then mind the law.
